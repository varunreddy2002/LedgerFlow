LedgerFlow · Engineering explainer

A double-entry ledger, and the ladder that fills it

How a raw bank row becomes a balanced, auditable journal entry — deterministic where money is involved, AI only where judgment is, and never posted without a gate.

The one-paragraph version

What the system does

Every financial fact is stored as a balanced journal entry: equal debits and credits, following 500-year-old double-entry accounting. Raw bank rows come in uncategorized. An escalation ladder decides, per row, which account the other side of the entry belongs to — trying cheap deterministic rules first, an AI agent next, and a human last. The ledger then reads those posted entries to produce the official numbers (P&L, trial balance). Two rules never bend: money math is never done by the AI, and nothing hits the books without a high-confidence rule or a human approval.

Foundation

Double-entry in sixty seconds

A journal entry records one economic event as two-or-more lines. Each line is a debit or a credit — never both. The iron law: total debits must equal total credits. That is the only reason the books always balance.

Example — the business pays a $42 AWS bill from checking:

5330 · Cloud Hosting (expense)

Debit
Credit
42.00

1110 · Operating Checking (asset)

Debit
Credit
42.00

Debits 42 = credits 42 → balanced. The expense went up; the bank went down. Whether a debit increases or decreases an account depends on the account's normal balance — the single fact everything else derives from:

  • Assets & expenses are debit-normal → a debit increases them.
  • Liabilities, equity & revenue are credit-normal → a credit increases them.
Owner-confirmed posting rule (cash basis) Money in → Dr Cash / Cr Revenue. Money out → Cr Cash / Dr Expense. The cash side is always fixed by direction; only the counter account is classified by the ladder.

The data model

Three tables are the accounting

accounts

Chart of accounts

A 3-level tree. You post only to the leaves. Reports roll leaf balances up into groups and totals.

journal_entries

Entry header

One per event. Carries a status: draft → pending → posted. Only posted entries count in reports.

journal_entry_lines

The debit/credit lines

Each line is one side, one account. DB constraints enforce: never both sides, never empty.

JournalEntryBuilder

The gatekeeper

Refuses to build an entry unless it balances and every account is a posting leaf. Callers never touch raw signs.

The hierarchy — and where account_subtype comes from

Every account has a type (which statement it belongs to) and, one level down, a subtype — a machine-readable role owned by the level-2 group and inherited by its leaves. That is the clean, deterministic basis: the tree itself decides the subtype.

1000 Assets — type: asset 1100 Cash & Bank → subtype: CASH 1110 Operating Checking ◆ posting leaf 1120 Business Savings ◆ posting leaf 1200 Receivables → subtype: RECEIVABLE 1210 Accounts Receivable ◆ posting leaf · control 5000 Operating Expenses — type: expense 5300 Technology → subtype: TECHNOLOGY 5320 Software & SaaS ◆ posting leaf 5330 Cloud Hosting ◆ posting leaf

type answers “which financial statement?” subtype answers “which functional bucket within it?” — and it's derived straight from the level-2 parent, so it's 100% populated and never guessed.

ledger.py, demystified

The ledger: read-only, deterministic, tested

This is the file you found confusing. It is deliberately split into two roles so the accounting math has zero dependency on the database and can be unit-tested with fakes:

SqlAlchemyLedgerRepository

Fetches numbers

Talks to Postgres. Returns plain records: the list of accounts, and the summed debit/credit per account over posted entries. No math, no signs.

LedgerService

Does the math

Pure functions over those numbers. Applies the sign rule, rolls the tree up, and returns typed reports. Depends on the repository interface, not the DB.

Step 1 — the one sign rule (lives in exactly one place)

signed_balance(account, totals): if account is debit-normal → debit − credit if account is credit-normal → credit − debit

A positive result means the account sits on its normal side. Cash with 1000 debits and 300 credits → +700 (an asset balance of 700).

Step 2 — roll the leaves up the tree

Each leaf gets its signed balance; then children are summed into parents, deepest level first. A group's balance is just the sum of its leaves — no double counting, because only leaves hold postings.

flowchart TD
  A["5320 Software 500"] --> G["5300 Technology 800"]
  B["5330 Cloud 300"] --> G
  G --> T["5000 Expenses (total)"]

Step 3 — four primitives, everything else derives from them

  • balance(account) — one account's rolled-up balance.
  • account_tree() — the whole COA with balances at every node.
  • pnl(start, end) — revenue subtree − expense subtree = net income.
  • trial_balance(as_of) — every account's ending balance; asserts Σdebits = Σcredits.
Why the split matters Because LedgerService depends on an interface, its tests inject a fake repository with canned numbers — so the P&L rollup and the balancing assertion are proven with no database at all. The money math is the trusted core; it must be identical every time.

The escalation ladder

How a bank row gets classified

The ladder is a Chain of Responsibility: three handlers in a line. Each one either resolves the row or passes it down. Cheapest and most certain first; human last. This is the pattern to show your lead — it's why adding a new step later never touches the existing ones.

  RULE -->|"no match"| AGENT

  REVIEW["3 · ReviewHandler
create review item"] --> HUMAN["Human approves in chat

  HUMAN --> POST2["Build + post entry"]
  POST2 -.->|"correction becomes a new rule"| RULE
  POST --> BOOKS(("On the books"))
  POST2 --> BOOKS

What each rung decides

post rule match · conf ≥ 0.75 · amount ≤ $5,000 Auto-posts a balanced entry. Fast, free, auditable.
review rule match · amount > $5,000 Large-amount override — big money always sees a human.
review rule match · confidence < 0.75 Parked for review, carrying the rule's suggestion.
review no rule → agent picks an account Agent proposes; still goes to a human. It never posts.
The bright line The AI only ever chooses which account. It never computes an amount and never decides the balance — those come from the bank row and the builder. And nothing reaches the books without either a high-confidence deterministic rule or a human clicking approve.

The flywheel

When a human corrects a review item, that correction is written back as a new deterministic rule. So the same vendor auto-posts next time. The rule layer grows, the agent is needed less, and every decision is traceable to why the system remembers it.
